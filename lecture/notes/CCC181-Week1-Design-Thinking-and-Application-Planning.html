Design thinking

Five stages, one loop

1 · Today

Empathize

Observe, interview and listen to real users in context

2 · Today

Define

Turn what you learned into one clear problem statement

3 · Today

Ideate

Generate many possible solutions before choosing one

4 · Later

Prototype

Build cheap, quick versions to make ideas tangible

5 · Later

Test

Put prototypes before users, learn, and loop back

Application planning lives in stages 1–3. It is not linear: a failed test often sends you back to Define, or even to Empathize.

04

Empathize

Two sets of needs to satisfy

User needs

The people who use the app

  • Goals: what they are trying to get done
  • Pain points: what slows or frustrates them now
  • Context: device, connectivity, time, place
  • Accessibility: vision, language, motor, literacy
  • Trust: privacy, reliability, sense of control

Organizational needs

The institution that owns and runs it

  • Mission: goals the app must advance
  • Compliance: policy and law, e.g. the Data Privacy Act of 2012
  • Resources: budget, staff, support capacity
  • Systems: existing data and tools to integrate
  • Reporting: what leaders need to decide

Where they collide: students want the fewest steps; the clinic wants complete records. Good planning finds a version that serves both.

06

Define

Problem definition

Point-of-view statement

[User] needs [need] because [insight].

Case example

A working student with back-to-back classes needs to see a nurse without losing a whole morning, because the clinic takes walk-ins only and the queue is unpredictable.

How might we make clinic wait times predictable for students with tight schedules?

A good statement…

names a real user group

states a need, not a feature

rests on an insight from research

is narrow enough to act on, open enough for ideas

Not a problem: “We need a mobile app.” That is a solution.

Define

Value proposition

Template

For [target users] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [current alternative], it [differentiator].

Case example

For students with tight schedules who need clinic care, ClinicQ is a web app that shows their live place in the queue and alerts them when their turn is near. Unlike the walk-in line, it lets them wait anywhere.

Plan

Requirements

Functional: what it does

As a [user], I want [action] so that [benefit].

  • As a student, I want to join the queue remotely so that I can wait in class
  • As a nurse, I want to call the next student in one tap

Non-functional: how well

  • Performance: queue position updates within 5 s
  • Usability: join the queue in 3 taps or fewer
  • Privacy: only clinic staff see the reason for visit
  • Compatibility: works on low-end Android and slow data

Prioritize with MoSCoW

Must

Join queue, see position, get alert

Should

Staff queue dashboard

Could

Visit history for students

Won't (this time)

Online consultations

MoSCoW: prioritizing requirements

M

Must have

Without it, the app fails or doesn't solve the problem. One missing Must means the release fails.

ClinicQ: join queue, see position, get alert

S

Should have

Important and valuable, but the app still works without it. Can wait for the next release.

ClinicQ: staff queue dashboard

C

Could have

Nice extras, added only if time allows. The first to be cut when the schedule slips.

ClinicQ: visit history for students

W

Won't have (this time)

Agreed out of scope for now. Writing it down is what stops scope creep.

ClinicQ: online consultations

Rule of thumb: if the app still solves your problem statement without a feature, it is not a Must. Keep the Must list short.

Created by Dai Clegg at Oracle (1994); later adopted by the DSDM agile method

11

Plan

Scope: drawing the boundary

In scope (MVP)

  • Sign in with school email
  • Join queue, see position
  • Alert when 3 people ahead
  • Staff queue screen

Out of scope

  • Online consultations
  • Full medical records
  • Payments
  • Native iOS and Android apps

Constraints

  • 3-person team, one semester
  • Free or low-cost hosting
  • Assumes a staff laptop at the front desk
  • Health data under RA 10173

Scope creep: every “small” extra borrows time from the Must list. Write the out-of-scope list down and get stakeholders to agree to it.

MVP: minimum viable product

Definition

The smallest version of the app that still solves the core problem for the target user, so real people can use it and you can learn from them.

Minimum

Only what the value proposition depends on: usually the Must list

Viable

It must work and deliver value; half-built features don't count

Not an MVP: one wheel of a car. It gets no one anywhere.

An MVP: a bicycle. It gets you across town today.

ClinicQ MVP

Sign in, join the queue, see your position, get an alert; plus a staff queue screen. Consultations, records and payments wait.

For your project: the MVP is the scope you commit to finishing this semester. 

Term coined by Frank Robinson (2001); popularized by Eric Ries, The Lean Startup (2011)

Emerging technology

Options, and when each fits

AI & LLMs

Fit: summarizing, triage, plain-language search

Risk: confident wrong answers; private data in prompts

Mobile & PWA

Fit: phone-first users; offline use; push alerts

Risk: native apps add store review and two codebases

Cloud & serverless

Fit: small teams, spiky traffic, fast launch

Risk: vendor lock-in; costs that grow unnoticed

IoT & sensors

Fit: physical-world data: rooms, queues, energy

Risk: hardware cost, installation, upkeep

AR / VR

Fit: spatial training, 3D visualization

Risk: few users own headsets; motion discomfort

Blockchain

Fit: shared records among parties who don't trust each other

Risk: rarely needed; a database usually suffices

Individual learning activity

Three parts, done on your own

01

Design-thinking activity

Observe a real problem on campus or in your community. Interview at least two people affected, build an empathy map, then write a POV statement and a “How might we…?” question.

Output: empathy map, POV, HMW

02

Application analysis

Pick an existing app that addresses a similar problem. Identify its target users, problem, value proposition and technology, then note where it falls short.

Output: one-page analysis with gaps

03

Project idea consultation

Bring your problem, target user and proposed app to your instructor. 

Output: approved or revised idea

Parts 1 and 2 feed your consultation; the consultation feeds your project milestone.
